Validating a browser-based lunar polar illumination model against NASA's LOLA visibility map

SUNLOCK technical note · 15 November 2026 · code: github.com/shahaman098/sunlock-app (earlier history, including the pre-registration commit 83b4287: github.com/shahaman098/sunlock)

Abstract

We test a browser-based lunar south-pole illumination model against NASA's published average-visibility product (AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608, PGDA product 69) — 18.6 years of hourly illumination computed by the LOLA team from their own terrain, with an independent pipeline. At 1,782 random sample points between 85°S and 90°S the model reproduces the reference with Pearson r = 0.990, RMSE 1.88 percentage points and mean bias +0.01 pp. At 105 published landing locations, which sit on crests and rims, the error is larger: RMSE 6.8 pp.

The comparison found a defect that no internal check had caught: the local horizon was being looked up 180° away from the Sun's azimuth, so every site was tested against terrain on the opposite side. Correcting it moved a permanently shadowed crater floor from 30.5% to 0.0% (reference: 0.0%) and raised the correlation across 60 diagnostics points from −0.18 to +0.98. Every illumination number produced by the model before this fix was wrong in a site-dependent way; the corrected values are reported here.

The astronomy is verified separately against JPL Horizons: sub-solar longitude agrees to 0.0032°, sun direction to 21″.

1. Introduction

Tools that predict polar illumination are only as good as their terrain handling. Published values for the best south-pole sites cluster at 85–88% (Mazarico et al. 2011; Gläser et al. 2017), but those figures depend on terrain resolution, ray-sampling interval, observer height, temporal baseline and whether the Sun's disc centre or any part of its disc must clear the horizon.

Internal consistency does not establish correctness. This note reports an external test against a reference product we did not produce, and the defect that test exposed.

The reference defines its metric explicitly: "the percentage of timesteps where each pixel was sunlit (by any fraction of the solar disc)", scaling DN × 4×10⁻⁵, over 18.6 years.

3. Method

For each sample point we ray-march a 360-azimuth horizon on the 80 m elevation model to a 150 km reach, subtracting the lunar curvature drop d²/2R. Sun and Earth directions come from Astronomy Engine, rotated into the IAU 2015 lunar frame. A point is illuminated when any part of the 0.266° solar disc clears the horizon in the Sun's azimuth.

Sampling: fixed seed, 2,000 points uniform in area over 85–90°S plus the nine named regions; points falling outside valid data are dropped and counted.

Bias by band: +0.15 pp (0–20% illumination, 737 points), −0.08 pp (20–50%, 1,042 points). Only 3 points fall in 50–80% and none above, so the random sample says little about the sunniest ground. Agreement is not carried by one end of the range it does cover.

The app ships the converged profile for its named sites (bilinear 40 m steps, 150 km reach). A point you type in is traced on the coarser 594 m elevation grid at half-cell steps out to 150 km. The older 160 m nearest-neighbour profile, still labelled shipped in the result files, scores r = 0.991 and RMSE 1.79 pp on 270 points of a 300-point, 3-hourly run; it is kept only for comparison and is no longer the profile the app uses.

Landing sites. At 105 published Artemis III landing locations the same comparison gives r = 0.915, RMSE 6.8 pp, bias −1.1 pp, with 80 of 105 within 5 pp (web/public/terrain/validation.json). These are the sunniest crests, exactly where the random sample is thin, and where sunlight changes over tens of metres.

Deviation from the pre-registration. The converged convention uses a 150 km horizon reach, decided after H1 was first run; the pre-registered reach was 64 km, which the older shipped convention keeps.

With the app's "any part of the disc" rule, state agreement is 95.4% and the median timing error 3.3 h (our sunrise early, our sunset late): a photo pixel reads bright only with substantial direct light. Threshold sensitivity: 97.5% at half the threshold, 92.8% at double.

Both photograph tests use a binary light/dark rule (half the disc, then any part of it), so they check the lighting decision and not the fractional-disc power calculation the app uses for generation. They also cover December 2009 within 24 km of the pole and 7-day stays only, so they say nothing directly about the 14-day February stays in the showcase.

Limits: one month in southern summer; a 100 m pixel on a slope facing away from a grazing Sun reads dark while the Sun is above the skyline; 14-day stays could not be tested because the month has no 14-day run of frames without a gap over 6 hours; survival here tests the shadow timing fed to the power model, not the power model. 4.2 The defect the test found

An earlier build looked up the horizon at the azimuth 180° from the Sun. The error is invisible at the pole (where terrain is near-isotropic) and survives spot checks on summits, but fails decisively in a crater:

The cause is an axis convention: this PDS polar-stereographic product stores lines and samples opposite to the standard stereographic x/y convention, so the image-space direction must be negated. The fix is in scripts/build-terrain.mjs and web/src/lib/terrain.ts.

4.3 Consequence for every published number

With the horizon corrected, the peak near Shackleton yields 73% illumination over 1 Jan – 30 Jun 2027 (previously 86%), with a longest continuous darkness of 11.5 days (previously 3.5). The 73% is a region centre, not a searched optimum; published values of 85–88% refer to clusters 20–40 m wide found by search (Gläser et al. 2017).

6. Sensitivity

EffectMagnitude
Finite solar disc vs centre-of-disc≤ 0.26%
Landing point moved ±250 m (horizon fixed)σ ≤ 0.08%
Horizon azimuth sampled every 4° instead of 1°≤ 0.24%
Ray interval 80 → 320 m60.6% → 81.8%

Ray-sampling interval is the largest remaining sensitivity. Coarse intervals step over ridges and inflate sunlight. The converged 40 m bilinear profile the app uses is the one that scores r = 0.990 (1.88 pp RMSE) against NASA over 1,782 points, which is its empirical justification.

7. Limitations

  • Accuracy at a specific landing point is several times worse than map-wide (6.8 pp against

1.88 pp RMSE), so a survival verdict at a named site is an early-planning estimate.

  • Mission survival is checked against photographs only for 7-day stays in one month (§4.1b);

14-day stays are untested.

  • The 180° azimuth defect was found and fixed using the same NASA map that H1 is scored

against. The thresholds were fixed beforehand, but H1 is not independent of that fix; H3 (photographs) is.

  • Re-running §4.1 needs NASA's raw LOLA elevation model, which is not stored in the repository.
  • One hemisphere-season (180 days) in the product; the reference spans 18.6 years.
  • No thermal, dust or power-system modelling. Array output is first-order.
  • Physical libration enters only through the IAU series.

8. Conclusions

1. A browser-resident model, using public terrain and ephemerides, reproduces NASA's 18.6-year polar visibility map to r = 0.990 and 1.9 percentage points RMSE map-wide, and to 6.8 points at published landing sites. 2. External validation found a 180° azimuth defect that internal checks and spot checks had missed. Reference products are worth more than self-consistency. 3. Illumination at a single point remains sensitive to ray interval and site choice; the number is only meaningful with its convention stated, and the map-wide test is the honest way to choose that convention.
